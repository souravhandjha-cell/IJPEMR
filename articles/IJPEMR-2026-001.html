 Development and Optimization of Glibenclamide-Loaded Chitosan Microspheres in a
    Thermosensitive Nasal In-Situ Gel: In-Vitro Formulation and Characterization

Abstract
Background: Glibenclamide  is a potent sulfonylurea whose formulation performance  is
constrained by poor aqueous solubility and variable oral absorption. A particulate-in-gel nasal
formulation was investigated as a formulation strategy to improve drug retention and provide
controlled in-vitro release.
Objective:  To  formulate  glibenclamide-loaded  chitosan  microspheres,  optimize  the
formulation using a 3² full factorial design, and incorporate the optimized microspheres into a
thermosensitive in-situ gel for physicochemical and in-vitro performance characterization.
Methods: Chitosan microspheres were prepared by a water-in-oil emulsion cross-linking
process using glutaraldehyde. Chitosan concentration (X1) and glutaraldehyde volume (X2)
were evaluated at three levels. Particle size (Y1), entrapment efficiency (Y2), and 8-h
cumulative drug release (Y3) were used as formulation responses. The optimized formulation
was characterized for particle size, polydispersity, zeta potential, release kinetics, sol-to-gel
transition temperature, mucoadhesive strength, and viscosity.
Results: The reported optimized formulation F5 contained 1.5% w/v chitosan and 2.0 mL
glutaraldehyde and showed a mean particle diameter of 42.35 ± 1.20 µm, entrapment efficiency
of 84.62 ± 1.85%, and 8-h drug release of 81.15 ± 1.40%. The reported regression model for
entrapment efficiency showed R² = 0.9930, adjusted R² = 0.9813, and adequate precision of
28.42. The in-situ gel showed a sol-to-gel transition temperature of 33.4 ± 0.3 °C and reported
mucoadhesive strength of 4250 ± 112 dynes/cm². The release profile was best fitted by the
Korsmeyer–Peppas model (R² = 0.9945; n = 0.62).
Conclusion: The reported data support successful formulation optimization and favorable in-
vitro physicochemical performance of the microsphere-loaded thermosensitive gel. No animal,
human-subject, animal-derived tissue, or human-derived biological material was used in this
revised  study  scope;  therefore,  conclusions  are  limited  to  formulation  and  in-vitro
characterization.
Keywords: Glibenclamide; chitosan microspheres; thermosensitive in-situ gel; Poloxamer
407; PEG 400; factorial design; drug release; Korsmeyer–Peppas kinetics.
1. Introduction
Type 2 diabetes mellitus is a chronic metabolic disorder requiring sustained pharmacological
management. Glibenclamide (GLB), a second-generation sulfonylurea, acts primarily by
stimulating insulin release through interaction with ATP-sensitive potassium channels in

Page 2 of 10
International Journal of Pharmaceutical, Engineering and Multidisciplinary Research (IJPEMR)

pancreatic  beta  cells.  Although  therapeutically  potent,  formulation  and  absorption
characteristics can limit the consistency of oral drug delivery.
The source manuscript describes glibenclamide as a poorly water-soluble, lipophilic drug and
identifies dissolution and variable systemic exposure as important formulation challenges.
Improving the microenvironment around the drug and controlling its release are therefore
relevant formulation objectives.
Intranasal administration is attractive from a dosage-form development perspective because
the nasal cavity provides a highly vascularized mucosal surface and can support rapid drug
uptake. However, a conventional liquid preparation may be removed rapidly by mucociliary
clearance. A formulation that combines particulate drug entrapment with temperature-triggered
gel formation may increase residence and maintain a more controlled release environment.
Chitosan was selected as the microsphere-forming polymer because its protonatable amino
groups can provide mucoadhesive interactions with negatively charged mucosal surfaces.
Cross-linking with glutaraldehyde can produce a particulate polymeric matrix capable of
entrapping drug. Poloxamer 407 (Pluronic F-127) was selected as the thermoresponsive gel-
forming polymer, with PEG 400 included as a formulation component.
The present revised manuscript deliberately focuses on formulation development and in-vitro
characterization. Animal pharmacokinetic, pharmacodynamic, histopathological, and animal-
derived ex-vivo tissue experiments from the earlier source version are excluded from the study
scope rather than replaced by invented experiments.
2. Materials and Methods
2.1 Study design and scope
This was a formulation-development and in-vitro characterization study. The experimental
scope  comprised  preparation  of  glibenclamide-loaded  chitosan  microspheres,  factorial
optimization, physicochemical characterization, incorporation into a thermosensitive in-situ
gel, and in-vitro drug-release and rheological evaluation. No animal study, human study,
animal-derived tissue study, or human-derived biological material was included in the revised
scope.
2.2 Materials
The source manuscript reported glibenclamide (USP, >99.2% purity), low-molecular-weight
chitosan (degree of deacetylation >85%; molecular weight approximately 150 kDa), Pluronic
F-127 (Poloxamer 407), polyethylene glycol 400 (PEG 400), and glutaraldehyde solution (25%
v/v). Light liquid paraffin, Span 80, and glacial acetic acid were also reported. The source

  2.3 Preparation of glibenclamide-loaded chitosan microspheres
 A water-in-oil emulsion cross-linking procedure was reported. Chitosan at 1.0–2.0% w/v was
  dissolved in 1.5% v/v aqueous acetic acid. Glibenclamide (100 mg) was dissolved in 2 mL
  methanol and mixed with the polymeric phase under probe sonication for 2 min at 40 kHz. The
  aqueous phase was added dropwise to 50 mL light liquid paraffin containing 1.0% w/v Span
  80 and stirred at 1800 rpm for 30 min to form the emulsion.
  Glutaraldehyde solution at 1.0–3.0 mL was added dropwise, followed by cross-linking at
  40 °C for 3.5 h. Microspheres were isolated by centrifugation at 4500 rpm for 15 min, washed
  three times with petroleum ether to remove paraffin, washed with deionized water, and
  vacuum-dried at 40 °C for 24 h.

2.6 Preparation of thermosensitive in-situ gel
The optimized microspheres were incorporated into a thermosensitive gel matrix based on
Pluronic F-127 at 18% w/v and PEG 400 at 1.5% w/v, as reported in the source manuscript.
The formulation was evaluated for sol-to-gel transition temperature, mucoadhesive strength,
and viscosity under different temperature and shear-rate conditions.
2.7 Particle size, polydispersity and zeta potential
The optimized microspheres were characterized for mean particle diameter, polydispersity
index (PDI), and zeta potential. The reported values were 42.35 ± 2.15 µm, PDI 0.214, and
+28.4 ± 1.8 mV, respectively. The source formulation matrix reported 42.35 ± 1.20 µm for F5;
this difference in reported standard deviation is retained transparently rather than silently
reconciled.
2.8 In-vitro drug release and kinetic modeling
Cumulative in-vitro drug release from the optimized formulation was evaluated and fitted to
commonly used mathematical models. The source manuscript reported fitting to zero-order,
first-order, Higuchi, Korsmeyer–Peppas, and Hixson–Crowell models.

2.10 Statistical analysis
Factorial responses were analyzed using a second-order polynomial response-surface approach
and ANOVA. Statistical significance was interpreted using the p-values reported in the source
manuscript. For the entrapment-efficiency model, the reported model F-value was 84.58 with
p < 0.0001, R² = 0.9930, adjusted R² = 0.9813, and adequate precision = 28.42.
3. Results
3.1 Effect of formulation variables on microsphere characteristics
The nine factorial runs demonstrated a progressive increase in particle size with increasing
chitosan concentration and glutaraldehyde level. Particle size ranged from 32.14 ± 1.20 µm in
F1 to 58.90 ± 2.10 µm in F9. Entrapment efficiency increased across the design space from
62.30 ± 1.40% in F1 to 91.10 ± 1.90% in F9. In contrast, the 8-h cumulative release generally
decreased as polymer and cross-linker levels increased, ranging from 92.40 ± 2.10% in F1 to
52.80 ± 1.30% in F9.

  The optimized microspheres exhibited a mean particle diameter of approximately 42 µm, PDI

4. Discussion
The formulation data demonstrate the expected relationship between polymer/cross-linker
content and microsphere structure. Increasing chitosan concentration increases the amount of
polymer available to form the microsphere matrix, which is associated in the reported design
space with larger  particle  size and greater drug entrapment. Increasing glutaraldehyde
concentration likewise contributes to matrix cross-linking and  is associated with higher
entrapment and slower release in the reported factorial data.
The release results are consistent with a matrix-controlled system in which drug diffusion and
polymeric structural relaxation can both contribute to release. The Korsmeyer–Peppas model
produced the highest reported coefficient of determination, and the exponent of 0.62 was
classified in the source manuscript as non-Fickian/anomalous release. This interpretation
should be regarded as a mathematical description of the reported release curve rather than proof
of a specific molecular release mechanism.
The thermosensitive gel provides a second formulation-control element. At room temperature
the formulation remains comparatively less viscous, whereas the reported transition near
33.4 °C is accompanied by a large increase in viscosity. Such a transition can be useful for
dosage-form handling and retention; however, actual nasal residence time, mucociliary
clearance, permeation enhancement, and systemic bioavailability cannot be established from
the present in-vitro data alone.
The positive zeta potential of the optimized microspheres is also relevant to formulation
characterization because chitosan-based systems can exhibit cationic surface characteristics.
Nevertheless, surface charge measurements alone do not establish mucoadhesion or enhanced
epithelial permeability in vivo. The present conclusions are therefore intentionally limited to
the measured formulation attributes.
5. Study limitations
The revised study intentionally excludes animal and human experimentation. Accordingly,
pharmacokinetic exposure, pharmacodynamic glucose lowering, systemic bioavailability,
histopathological safety, and in-vivo nasal residence cannot be concluded from this manuscript.
Animal-derived ex-vivo mucosal permeation data from the earlier source version are also
excluded because the present study scope is non-animal and non-human.

6. Conclusion
Glibenclamide-loaded chitosan microspheres were reported to be successfully prepared and
optimized using a 3² factorial design. The identified F5 formulation contained 1.5% w/v
chitosan and 2.0 mL glutaraldehyde and showed a reported particle size of 42.35 ± 1.20 µm,
entrapment efficiency of 84.62 ± 1.85%, and 8-h cumulative release of 81.15 ± 1.40%.
Incorporation into an 18% w/v Pluronic F-127/1.5% w/v PEG 400 thermosensitive gel
produced a reported sol-to-gel transition temperature of 33.4 ± 0.3 °C and mucoadhesive
strength  of  4250 ±  112  dynes/cm².  Release  kinetics  were  best  described  by  the
Korsmeyer–Peppas model. The revised manuscript supports formulation-level and in-vitro
conclusions only and does not claim animal or human efficacy, safety, permeation, or
bioavailability.

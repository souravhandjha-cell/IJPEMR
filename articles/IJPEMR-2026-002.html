ABSTRACT

Background: Diabetes mellitus is a metabolic disorder of rising global prevalence, and India carries one of the largest burdens of the disease. Medicinal plants are widely used in folk medicine as low-cost alternatives or adjuncts to synthetic antidiabetic drugs. Luffa echinata Roxb. (Cucurbitaceae) is traditionally used for several ailments, including diabetes.

Objective: To prepare a hydroalcoholic extract of L. echinata leaves, screen it for phytoconstituents, estimate its total phenolic and flavonoid content, and evaluate its antihyperglycemic activity in alloxan-induced diabetic rats.

Methods: Shade-dried leaves were defatted with petroleum ether and extracted by maceration with ethanol:water (70:20) for 48 h. Preliminary phytochemical screening was carried out by standard qualitative tests. Total phenolic content was estimated by the Folin-Ciocalteu method (gallic acid standard) and total flavonoid content by the aluminium chloride method (quercetin standard). Acute oral toxicity was assessed following OECD guideline 423. Diabetes was induced in Wistar rats with a single intraperitoneal dose of alloxan monohydrate (120 mg/kg). Diabetic rats received the extract at 100 and 200 mg/kg orally, or glibenclamide (600 µg/kg), for 15 days.

1. Introduction

Diabetes mellitus is a group of heterogeneous metabolic disorders characterised by hyperglycaemia, which results from defects in insulin secretion, insulin action, or both (Sicree et al., 2006). Chronic hyperglycaemia is associated with progressive damage to the eyes,

kidneys, nerves, heart and blood vessels, and it increases the risk of cardiovascular, peripheral vascular and cerebrovascular disease (WHO, 1999; Sicree et al., 2006).

The disease is classified into type 1 diabetes, type 2 diabetes, gestational diabetes and other specific types (Sicree et al., 2006). Type 2 diabetes accounts for roughly 85 to 95 percent of cases in high-income countries and an even larger share in developing countries (WHO, 1994). Its rising prevalence is linked to ageing populations, urbanisation, dietary change and reduced physical activity (WHO, 1994). The International Diabetes Federation estimated about 285 million adults with diabetes worldwide in 2010, a figure projected to reach 438 million by 2030 (Sicree et al., 2006).

India has the largest number of people with diabetes of any country. The estimate for 2000 was about 31.7 million, and projections for 2030 approached 79 million (Wild et al., 2004; Whiting et al., 2011). Diabetes also imposes a heavy economic burden through treatment costs, disability, premature mortality and lost productivity (Kirigia et al., 2009).

Current treatments include insulin, sulfonylureas, biguanides such as metformin, meglitinides, and lifestyle measures such as diet and exercise (Belinda, 2004; Votey and Peters, 2004). Plant- based medicines are attracting renewed interest because they are relatively inexpensive, widely available and, in many cases, have fewer side effects than synthetic drugs. India alone is estimated to have around 2,500 medicinal plant species, of which about 150 are used commercially on a large scale (Modak et al., 2007). Numerous plants have been screened for antidiabetic activity in alloxan- and streptozotocin-induced rodent models (Venter et al., 2008; Kar et al., 2003).

Luffa echinata Roxb. is a spreading climber of the family Cucurbitaceae, found in Pakistan, India, Bangladesh and northern tropical Africa. In India it occurs mainly in Gujarat, Bihar, Rajasthan and Madhya Pradesh (Prakash et al., 2013). The plant has a long history in traditional medicine for jaundice, colic, bronchitis, dropsy and diabetes (Modi and Kumar, 2014). Earlier studies have reported antiarthritic, hepatoprotective, antioxidant and hypoglycaemic activities of different parts of the plant (Chandel et al., 2013; Ahmed et al., 2001; Kumar et al., 2000). The hydroalcoholic extract has also been reported to produce hypoglycaemic action in rats (Sharma et al., 2012).

Despite these reports, the antidiabetic potential of the leaves has not been studied in detail. The present study therefore aimed to prepare a hydroalcoholic extract of L. echinata leaves, screen

it for phytoconstituents, quantify its phenolic and flavonoid content, and evaluate its antihyperglycaemic activity in alloxan-induced diabetic rats.

2. Review of Literature

2.1 Luffa echinata

Modi and Kumar (2014) reviewed the ethnomedicinal, phytochemical and pharmacological properties of L. echinata. The plant has been traditionally used against jaundice, intestinal colic, enlarged liver and spleen, leprosy, diabetes, bronchitis, nephritis, rheumatism and fever. Reported pharmacological activities include antioxidant, analgesic, anti-inflammatory, antidepressant, anxiolytic, antiepileptic, hepatoprotective, antibacterial, antifungal, antiulcer and anticancer effects.

Chandel et al. (2013) studied the aqueous and alcoholic extracts of L. echinata fruit in Freund’s complete adjuvant-induced arthritis in rats. Both extracts reduced paw oedema and restored haematological parameters, with the effect at 250 mg/kg reported as comparable across the developing and developed phases of arthritis. Ahmed et al. (2001) evaluated fruit extracts against CCl₄-induced hepatotoxicity and reported that petroleum ether, acetone and methanolic extracts gave hepatoprotection comparable to silymarin.

2.2 Plants Investigated for Antidiabetic Activity

A large number of plants have been tested for antidiabetic activity in experimental animals. Studies on Caralluma sinaica (Habibuddin et al., 2008), Phyllanthus reticulatus (Kumar et al., 2008), Murraya koenigii (Kesari et al., 2006; Yadav et al., 2002), Caesalpinia bonducella (Kannur et al., 2006), Aegle marmelos (Kesari et al., 2006) and Catharanthus roseus (Srinivas et al., 2003; Singh et al., 2001) reported dose-dependent reductions in blood glucose. Eidi et al. (2006) showed that garlic ethanolic extract lowered serum glucose, lipids and transaminases in streptozotocin-induced rats. Chattopadhyay (1999) compared four medicinal plants and found Azadirachta indica leaf extract to have the most potent blood-sugar-lowering effect, followed by C. roseus, Gymnema sylvestre and Ocimum sanctum. Several of these studies attributed the effect partly to antioxidant activity, which supports testing phenolic and flavonoid content alongside glucose-lowering activity (Raut and Gaikwad, 2006; Kamalakkannan and Prince, 2006).

3. Research Envisaged

Diabetes mellitus can be managed with diet, exercise and drugs, but synthetic agents are not free of adverse effects, and plant extracts remain an important source of alternatives (Votey and Peters, 2004). The aim of this study was to assess the extraction, phytochemical screening and in vivo antidiabetic activity of L. echinata leaf extract.

The specific objectives were:

1. To prepare a hydroalcoholic extract of L. echinata leaves and determine its percentage

yield.

2. To perform qualitative phytochemical screening for major phytoconstituents.

3. To estimate total phenolic and total flavonoid content.

4. To assess acute oral toxicity of the extract in rats.

5. To evaluate the antihyperglycaemic activity of the extract at two doses in alloxan-

induced diabetic rats, using glibenclamide as the reference drug.

4.4 Estimation of Total Phenolic Content

The total phenolic content was determined by a modified Folin-Ciocalteu method (Olajuyigbe and Afolayan, 2011). A gallic acid stock solution (50 mg in 50 ml methanol) was diluted to obtain standards in the range of 5 to 25 µg/ml. The extract (10 mg in 10 ml methanol, filtered; 1 mg/ml) was prepared in the same way. For the assay, 2 ml of each standard or extract was mixed with 1 ml of Folin-Ciocalteu reagent (diluted 1:10 with distilled water) and 1 ml of sodium carbonate (7.5 g/l). The mixture was vortexed for 15 s and kept for 15 min for colour development. Absorbance was read at 765 nm. A calibration curve was plotted, and the results were expressed as mg gallic acid equivalent (GAE) per gram of extract.

4.5 Estimation of Total Flavonoid Content

Total flavonoids were estimated by the aluminium chloride colourimetric method (Olajuyigbe and Afolayan, 2011). A quercetin stock (50 mg in 50 ml methanol) was diluted to 5 to 25 µg/ml. For the assay, 1 ml of 2% AlCl₃ solution was added to 3 ml of standard or extract solution, and

the mixture was left at room temperature for 15 min. Absorbance was measured at 420 nm, and results were expressed as mg quercetin equivalent (QE) per gram of extract.

4.6 Animals

Animals were acclimatised for 7 days before the experiments. All procedures were carried out between 08:00 and 15:00 h in a noise-free room. Each experiment used a separate group of six rats. 6. Discussion

The present study prepared a hydroalcoholic extract of L. echinata leaves and evaluated it for phytochemical content and antihyperglycaemic activity in alloxan-induced diabetic rats. Alloxan selectively destroys pancreatic beta cells, which leads to insulin deficiency and sustained hyperglycaemia (Szkudelski, 2012). The model is therefore suitable for screening agents that lower blood glucose through insulin-dependent or insulin-independent mechanisms.
